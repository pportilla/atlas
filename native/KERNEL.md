Throughout, $R=\mathbf Q[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ has a fixed monomial order. We write $\operatorname{lm}(f)$ for the leading monomial of $f$ and $L(I)$ for the ideal generated by the leading monomials of all nonzero elements of $I$. Geometric statements are over $\overline{\mathbf Q}$.

<a id="1-representation"></a>

## Representation

A polynomial is stored as a primitive integer polynomial, its terms sorted in decreasing order. Every step (S-polynomial, reduction step) is carried out up to a nonzero rational factor with exact integers (FLINT `fmpz`), so no rounding or modular approximation enters. The statements used below (membership in an ideal, divisibility of leading monomials) do not change when a polynomial is multiplied by a nonzero rational number.

The packed representation uses 16 bits per exponent and a total degree below $2^{15}$. A Gröbner problem exceeding these limits uses the unpacked FLINT polynomial ring, including for reductions against an existing basis.

Orders: graded reverse lexicographic, lexicographic, and "first variable, then graded reverse lexicographic", which is an elimination order for the first variable.

<a id="2-gröbner-bases"></a>

## Gröbner bases

Two basis elements can give different first steps in a division. Their S-polynomial cancels their leading terms at their least common multiple. If the remainder is nonzero, its leading monomial tells us which reduction the current basis is missing. Buchberger's algorithm adjoins the remainder and tests new pairs until all such ambiguities resolve.

Atlas uses the Gebauer–Möller criteria to discard pairs whose reductions already follow from others [GM]. The update when a polynomial $h$ enters the basis $G$ is Algorithm UPDATE of [BW, §5.5].

1. candidate pairs $(h,g)$, $g\in G$, are formed;
2. $(h,g_1)$ is discarded when another candidate $(h,g_2)$ has $\operatorname{lcm}(h,g_2)$ properly dividing $\operatorname{lcm}(h,g_1)$; among candidates with the same lcm one is kept, and none if one of them has coprime leading monomials;
3. candidates with coprime leading monomials are discarded;
4. an old pair $(g_1,g_2)$ is discarded when $\operatorname{lm}(h)$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(g_1,g_2)$ and this lcm differs from $\operatorname{lcm}(g_1,h)$ and from $\operatorname{lcm}(g_2,h)$;
5. the elements $g$ with $\operatorname{lm}(h)\mid\operatorname{lm}(g)$ form no pairs with later elements.

`Engine::insert` does exactly this. In step 2 the code tests a candidate against all other candidates, also those already discarded; this discards the same pairs as UPDATE, because a discarded candidate was discarded by one whose lcm divides its own, and divisibility is transitive. The only freedom is which of several candidates with one lcm is kept, and UPDATE allows any.

**Correctness** is the theorem of [GM], proved in [BW, §5.5] for Algorithm GRÖBNERNEW2. Let $A$ be the set of all polynomials ever inserted. When no pair is left, every S-polynomial of two elements of $A$ can be written as a combination of elements of $A$ whose terms are all below the lcm of the two leading monomials: for a pair that was treated this is its reduction, and for a pair that was discarded or never formed it follows from treated pairs by the chain criterion. By Buchberger's criterion in this form [BW, §5.5], $A$ is a Gröbner basis of the ideal generated by the input, and so is the subset of its elements whose leading monomials are minimal. The argument holds for any order in which pairs are selected, and a reduction may use any elements of $A$. The three strategies below differ only in these choices.

**Termination.** A polynomial enters $G$ only when its leading monomial is divisible by no leading monomial of a current reducer, so the monomial ideal generated by the leading monomials grows strictly; by Dickson's lemma this happens finitely often, and each insertion creates finitely many pairs.

**Output.** [Engine::finish](groebner.cpp#L471) keeps the elements whose leading monomials are minimal for divisibility, reduces every tail completely, makes them monic and sorts them. The tails are reduced in increasing order of the leading monomials: a term of a tail can only be reduced by an element with a smaller leading monomial, and that element is then already reduced. This is the reduced Gröbner basis, which is unique [BW, §5.2]. So the result does not depend on the strategy, and it must coincide, element for element, with the one any other correct system computes.

<a id="21-strategies"></a>

### Strategies

- **Eager.** Pairs by sugar (the degree the S-polynomial would have if the input were homogenized), then by lcm. Each S-polynomial is reduced completely at once; among the reducers of a term the one with least sugar excess is used.
- **Normal.** Pairs by lcm; the reducer with the smallest leading monomial.
- **Lazy.** Pairs by sugar. The sugar $s$ of a polynomial plays the role of its degree in the homogenized computation, and a reducer $g$ of a term $m$ is *admissible* when $\deg m-\deg\operatorname{lm}(g)+s(g)\le s$. Tails are reduced by admissible reducers only. If the leading term has reducers but none is admissible, the sugar must rise to $s'=\min_g(\deg m-\deg\operatorname{lm}(g)+s(g))$; if a pair or a waiting polynomial of sugar below $s'$ is pending, the polynomial is put aside with sugar $s'$ and taken up again when its turn comes. The input polynomials enter through the same waiting list, in the order of their degrees. An element whose leading monomial is divisible by that of a newer element stops forming pairs (step 5), but stays a reducer until the newer element also has a sugar excess at most its own.

The engine starts with the eager strategy for the graded order and with the lazy one otherwise. If coefficients or work exceed a limit it first tries two routes that do not depend on the strategy: for an order that does not compare degrees first, the [Hilbert-driven route](#the-hilbert-driven-route), then the [modular route](#the-modular-route), which does not suffer from swelling coefficients. After that it restarts with the next strategy; after a full cycle the limits widen, and from the seventh cycle on there is none. A restart discards everything, so correctness is that of the route that finishes. Since the reduced basis is unique, every route returns the same polynomials.

<a id="deadlines"></a>

### Deadlines

A caller that can give up, such as a resolution rule with a time limit, sets a deadline for the computations of its thread (`DeadlineScope` in [groebner.hpp](groebner.hpp)). The engine checks it before each round of strategies, each generator and S-polynomial, each degree of the Hilbert-driven route, and each step of a modular trace and each batch of primes, and raises `Expired` when it has passed. Parallel steps (lifting and reconstructing modular coefficients, replaying primes, verifying a candidate) give their workers a copy of the deadline: the workers stop at the next item, and the calling thread raises `Expired` only after joining them, so no worker is abandoned. A computation that stops returns nothing, so a deadline changes when an answer arrives, never which answer. A scope also records that a computation inside it expired, so that a caller can treat any later answer as a timeout even if a step caught the exception and went on.

<a id="22-proofs-for-the-lazy-strategy"></a>

### Proofs for the lazy strategy

*Postponing is a choice of selection and reduction order.* A polynomial $p$ put aside is an S-polynomial partly reduced by elements of $G$. Taking it up later continues the same reduction with the basis of that moment, which Buchberger's algorithm permits: all that is used is that $S(f,g)$ reduces to the element finally inserted (or to zero) by elements that are, or were, in the basis.

*Reducers that no longer form pairs.* They are elements of $A$, and the correctness argument above allows a reduction to use any element of $A$.

*Termination.* (a) A polynomial is inserted only when no reducer divides its leading monomial, and the reducers generate, among leading monomials, the same monomial ideal as all elements ever inserted (an element stops being a reducer only when a reducer divides its leading monomial). So the argument above applies. (b) A postponed polynomial advances at each visit. It was put aside with sugar $s'$, the least sugar at which its leading monomial has an admissible reducer. For a fixed monomial this least sugar can only decrease afterwards: new reducers can only lower the minimum, and a reducer is removed only in favour of one that divides the same monomials with a sugar excess not larger. So at the next visit a reducer is admissible, and at least one step is made. Each step lowers the leading monomial, so a polynomial is visited finitely often.

<a id="23-the-declared-prefix"></a>

### The declared prefix

The caller may declare that the first $k$ generators are a Gröbner basis for the order in use. The pairs among them are then not formed. This is Buchberger's criterion applied to those pairs: their S-polynomials reduce to zero by the $k$ generators themselves. The kernel only uses this with bases it has itself computed, in two situations: a reduced basis to which new generators are added, and a graded reverse lexicographic basis of $\mathbf Q[x]$ moved to $\mathbf Q[t,x]$, where it remains a Gröbner basis because the order restricted to monomials without $t$ is the old one.

<a id="7-the-modular-route"></a>

## The modular route

[modular.cpp](modular.cpp) computes the same reduced basis through images modulo primes $p<2^{31}$. Nothing in it is probabilistic: the lifted basis is proved correct over $\mathbf Q$ before it is returned, and an unlucky prime can only cost time.

<a id="71-homogenization"></a>

### Homogenization

Each generator $f_k$ is replaced by its homogenization $f_k^h\in\mathbf Q[x,y]$, and $I'=(f_1^h,\dots,f_s^h)$. On $\mathbf Q[x,y]$ the order compares total degrees first and then uses the given order, $y$ being the last variable.

**Lemma 5.** If $G'$ is a homogeneous Gröbner basis of $I'$ for this order, then $\{g(x,1):g\in G'\}$ is a Gröbner basis of $I=(f_1,\dots,f_s)$ for the given order.

*Proof.* The terms of a homogeneous $g$ have the same degree and distinct $x$-parts, so $\operatorname{lm}(g)=x^a y^i$ with $x^a=\operatorname{lm}(g(x,1))$. Clearly $g(x,1)\in I$. Let $f\in I$, $f=\sum a_kf_k$. Homogenizing, $y^ef^h=\sum y^{e_k}a_k^hf_k^h\in I'$ for suitable exponents, so $\operatorname{lm}(y^ef^h)=y^{e'}\operatorname{lm}(f)$ is divisible by some $\operatorname{lm}(g)$, $g\in G'$, and then $\operatorname{lm}(f)$ is divisible by $\operatorname{lm}(g(x,1))$. $\square$

<a id="72-trace-replay-lifting"></a>

### Trace, replay, lifting

*Trace.* For the graded reverse lexicographic order on the CPU, [the F4 trace](#the-f4-trace) builds one matrix per degree instead. The trace described here serves the other orders and the devices. For one prime $p_0$ the reduced Gröbner basis $G_{p_0}$ of $I'_{p_0}=(\bar f_1^h,\dots,\bar f_s^h)\subset\mathbf F_{p_0}[x,y]$ is computed by [Buchberger’s algorithm](#gröbner-bases), which is valid over any field, degree by degree. Each S-polynomial is reduced in a vector indexed by the monomials that can occur; the list of monomials and of reducers depends only on leading monomials. For every S-polynomial with a nonzero remainder the row operations are appended to a *replay program*, together with the positions of the coefficients that were zero.

For a sufficiently large group of same-degree pairs, the tracer freezes the current basis and constructs the union of the monomial columns and shifted reducers once. Each S-polynomial reduces independently against this triangular table on CPU threads, Triton, or OpenCL. Row elimination on the remainders proceeds in increasing least-common-multiple order. A dependent row is a linear combination of earlier rows of the same degree and of shifted reducers with leading terms strictly smaller than its pair lcm. Once the earlier pairs have a standard representation, so does this pair. Independent pairs enter the ordinary trace in order. A size bound limits the temporary table, and a failed device launch can reuse the same table on the CPU. For the GPU, the edges of the triangular table can also be grouped by destination. The level of a column is one more than the largest level of its parents, with source columns at level zero. Columns within a level can therefore be evaluated in parallel from completed parent residues, with one output per column and row.

*Replay.* For another prime $q$ the program is executed on the images of the generators modulo $q$. It consists of additions of multiples of rows, so whatever it produces lies in $I'_q$. If a leading coefficient vanishes or a coefficient recorded as zero does not, the prime is dropped. If most primes are dropped the trace prime is the suspect and another one is taken. Different primes are independent. Native threads, Triton, and OpenCL can execute the same recorded operations. After a device screens trace rows, batches smaller than 16 primes and batches that fit in one wave of native threads (up to 32 primes) run on the CPU. Automatic trace dispatch uses a machine-local timing profile and keeps replay on the native threads. [Instruction format](modular_replay.hpp) · [Native CPU interpreter](modular_replay.cpp)

*Lifting.* To add a new image $a$ modulo $p$ to a residue $r$ modulo $M$, the kernel computes $r+M((a-r)M^{-1}\bmod p)$. The prefix modulus $M$ and its inverse modulo $p$ are shared by all coefficients. Native workers lift and reconstruct disjoint coefficient ranges. The reconstructed values are compared with one further prime. This gives a candidate $G\subset\mathbf Q[x,y]$, monic and homogeneous, with the leading monomials of $G_{p_0}$. No claim is made about it yet.

<a id="73-certification"></a>

### Certification

**Theorem 6** (Arnold [A], for homogeneous ideals; the proof is short and given here). Let $f_1^h,\dots,f_s^h$ be homogeneous with integer coefficients, $I'$ the ideal they generate, $p$ a prime and $G_p$ a Gröbner basis of $I'_p$. Let $G$ be a finite set of monic homogeneous polynomials whose coefficients have denominators prime to $p$, such that

(a) $G$ is a Gröbner basis of the ideal $J$ it generates,
(b) every $f_k^h$ reduces to zero by $G$,
(c) $G$ and $G_p$ have the same leading monomials.

Then $J=I'$, and $G$ is a Gröbner basis of $I'$.

*Proof.* Let $A=\mathbf Z_{(p)}$ and, for an ideal $K$ of $\mathbf Q[x,y]$, let $K_A=K\cap A[x,y]$ and $\bar K$ its image in $\mathbf F_p[x,y]$. Division by $G$ of an element of $J_A$ stays in $A[x,y]$, because the leading coefficients are $1$, and ends in zero by (a); reducing modulo $p$ shows that $\bar G$ is a Gröbner basis of $\bar J$, so $L(\bar J)$ is generated by the leading monomials of $G$. By (b), $I'\subset J$, hence $I'_p\subset\overline{I'}\subset\bar J$. By (c), $L(I'_p)=L(\bar J)$, and two ideals, one inside the other, with the same leading monomials are equal: $I'_p=\overline{I'}=\bar J$. So every $h\in J_A$ is congruent modulo $p$ to some $g\in I'_A$, and $(h-g)/p\in J_A$; that is, $J_A=I'_A+pJ_A$. All these ideals are homogeneous, so the same holds in each degree $d$, where $(J_A)_d$ is a finitely generated module over the local ring $A$. By Nakayama's lemma $(J_A)_d=(I'_A)_d$. Hence $J_A=I'_A$ and $J=I'$. $\square$

For (a), every S-polynomial retained by the [critical-pair criteria](#gröbner-bases) must reduce to zero. For (b), the same condition applies to the input generators. For large homogeneous candidates, the verifier groups both sets of rows by degree and shares the selected divisor and its shifted tail for each monomial. CPU workers reduce independent coefficient vectors by fraction-free integer arithmetic with FLINT. Any nonzero irreducible coefficient disproves the candidate. Small bases and symbolic tables that exceed the memory bounds use the sparse exact verifier. For (c), the kernel compares leading monomials with those at the trace prime and checks that the prime divides no denominator of $G$. If a check fails, more primes are used.

Homogenizing is necessary. For a prime $p$ let $I=(x,z)\cap(1-py,\,z-1)$, generated by $z(z-1)$, $x(z-1)$, $z+yz-(p+1)yz^2$ and $x+xy-(p+1)xyz$, none of whose coefficients is divisible by $p$. Modulo $p$ these generate $(x,z)$. The set $G=\{x,z\}$ satisfies (a), (b) and (c), and yet $(x,z)\ne I$: the component $V(1-py,z-1)$ has no points modulo $p$. The proof above breaks at the last step, which needs the grading. This ideal is one of the tests.

<a id="74-back-to-the-given-order"></a>

### Back to the given order

By Theorem 6 and Lemma 5 the polynomials $g(x,1)$, $g\in G$, are a Gröbner basis of $I$ for the given order. The exact engine then removes the redundant ones and reduces the tails (no pair is formed), which gives the reduced basis.

<a id="75-the-f4-trace"></a><a id="the-f4-trace"></a>

### The F4 trace

For the graded reverse lexicographic order, `f4_trace.cpp` computes the [modular trace](#72-trace-replay-lifting) with one matrix per degree, and replays it matrix by matrix. A solve that asks for a device, or whose order does not compare degrees first, keeps the [Buchberger trace](#72-trace-replay-lifting). A constant generator is also left to that trace. Generators that are not homogeneous and miss the Hilbert bound below may be traced as they are, without homogenizing ([the direct trace](#the-direct-trace)). [F4 trace](f4_trace.cpp) · [Benchmark](../benchmarks/groebner_f4.py)

**The matrix of a degree.** Let $G$ be the elements found in lower degrees. The rows to reduce in degree $d$ are the generators of degree $d$ and, for each least common multiple $m$ of a pair of that degree, the products $(m/\operatorname{lm} g)\,g$ of its members except one, which is the *pivot row* of the column $m$. Every other monomial of a row that a leading monomial of $G$ divides gets a pivot row $(m/\operatorname{lm} g)\,g$ as well, and so do the monomials these rows bring in. The pivot rows are monic with distinct leading columns. Subtracting multiples of them, largest column first, clears the pivot columns of any row. This map $N$ from rows to vectors on the columns without a pivot row is linear. The new elements of degree $d$ are the rows of the reduced echelon form of the span $W$ of the $N(r)$, for the rows $r$ to reduce. Their leading monomials have no divisor among the leading monomials of $G$. This is the algorithm F4 [F], with the pairs of section 2.

**Random combinations.** Reducing every row costs one pass over the pivot rows per row, and most rows give nothing new (88% for Katsura-10). The tracer reduces $k$ combinations $\sum_i a_{ji}r_i$ with coefficients drawn uniformly modulo $p$ instead. By linearity their images $\sum_i a_{ji}N(r_i)$ are independent uniform elements of $W$. When a matrix has few rows, they are reduced themselves and nothing is random.

**Lemma 8.** If $U\subsetneq W$ is a subspace, a uniform element of $W$ lies in $U$ with probability at most $1/p$. So $k\le\dim W$ uniform elements are independent with probability at least $1-1/(p-1)$, and when $v_1,\dots,v_k$ do not span $W$, a further uniform element depends on them with probability at most $1/p$.

*Proof.* $|U|/|W|\le 1/p$. The $i$-th element must avoid the span of the earlier ones, of dimension at most $i-1$ in a space of dimension at least $k$: the probability of failure is at most $\sum_{i\le k}p^{i-1-k}<1/(p-1)$. $\square$

The $k$ combinations are the *lanes* of one block: an array with $k$ entries per column. The pass over the pivot rows reads each row once and applies it to all lanes, with a 64-bit accumulator per entry kept below $p^2<2^{62}$. Blocks have at most 32 lanes and run on separate threads.

**How many combinations.** When the ideal has at most as many generators as variables, the number is known in advance.

**Lemma 9.** Let $f_1,\dots,f_s$ be forms of degrees $d_1,\dots,d_s\ge1$ in $S=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ over a field, with $s\le n$, and let $\sum_d c_dt^d=\prod_i(1-t^{d_i})/(1-t)^n$. Then $\dim_k(S/(f_1,\dots,f_s))_d\ge c_d$ for every $d$.

*Proof.* The dimensions do not change over the algebraic closure, so let $k$ be algebraically closed. Let $A$ be the affine space of all tuples of forms of these degrees. For a tuple $g$, $\dim(g_1,\dots,g_s)_d$ is the rank of the map $\bigoplus_iS_{d-d_i}\to S_d$, $(a_i)\mapsto\sum a_ig_i$, whose matrix has entries linear in the coordinates of $A$. So the tuples where this rank is at least a given number form an open set. The tuples that are regular sequences also form an open set: these are the tuples whose zero set in $\mathbf P^{n-1}$ has dimension $n-1-s$ [E, Ch. 18], and the dimension of the fibres of a projective family is upper semicontinuous [E, §14.3]. It is not empty, since it contains $(x_1^{d_1},\dots,x_s^{d_s})$. For a regular sequence the quotient has the Hilbert series $\prod_i(1-t^{d_i})/(1-t)^n$. If some tuple had $\dim(S/(f))_d<c_d$, the open set of tuples with a rank at least as large as its rank would meet the regular sequences, because $A$ is irreducible. That contradicts their Hilbert series. $\square$

Let $L$ be the monomial ideal generated by the leading monomials found so far, all of them leading monomials of elements of $I'_p$, the ideal of the homogenized generators modulo $p$. Then $\dim(S/L)_d\ge\dim(S/I'_p)_d\ge c_d$. So degree $d$ has at most $\tau_d=\dim(S/L)_d-c_d$ new leading monomials. The tracer computes $\tau_d$ from the numerator of the Hilbert series of $L$ (8.1) and reduces $\tau_d$ combinations.

- If they give $\tau_d$ new elements, then $\dim(S/L)_d=c_d$ afterwards, both inequalities are equalities, and the leading monomials of degree $d$ are complete. This is a proof. No randomness enters it.
- If $\tau_d=0$ the degree needs no matrix.
- If the Hilbert series of $S/L$ equals $\prod_i(1-t^{d_i})/(1-t)^n$, the same holds in every degree at once. By Theorem 7 the elements found are a Gröbner basis of $I'_p$, and the remaining pairs are dropped.
- If a combination depends on the earlier ones before $\tau_d$ is reached, the combinations are drawn once more. If the count is still short, the homogenized generators are no complete intersection, or the draw was unlucky. When the given generators are not homogeneous, the trace is given up and the choice of [the direct trace](#the-direct-trace) is made. Otherwise it continues with what it found, and treats the later degrees as if there were no bound. By Lemma 8 the rows found span $W$ except with probability at most $1/p$.

With more generators than variables there is no bound, and for generators that are not homogeneous the choice of [the direct trace](#the-direct-trace) is made. Without a bound, blocks are reduced, one, two, four, … at a time, until a combination depends on the earlier ones. By Lemma 8 they then span $W$ except with probability at most $1/p$ for each matrix.

For the Katsura ideals of the tests and benchmarks every degree meets its bound: their homogenized generators are a regular sequence.

**The reduced basis.** The minimal elements are the rows to reduce of a last matrix, each without its leading term, with a pivot row for every monomial a leading monomial divides. Reducing them gives the reduced basis $T$ modulo $p$.

**Correctness.** Every element the trace inserts is a combination of the generators modulo $p$: the rows are multiples of generators or of earlier elements, and elimination forms combinations of rows. So $T\subset I'_p$, whether or not the draws were lucky. This is all that the [certificate](#73-certification) uses.

**Proposition 10.** Theorem 6 holds with (c) replaced by: (c′) there is a finite set $T\subset I'_p$ whose leading monomials are those of $G$.

*Proof.* As in the proof of Theorem 6, (a) shows that $\bar G$ is a Gröbner basis of $\bar J$, so $L(\bar J)$ is generated by the leading monomials of $G$, and (b) gives $I'_p\subset\bar J$. Then
$$L(I'_p)\subset L(\bar J)=(\operatorname{lm}G)=(\operatorname{lm}T)\subset L(I'_p),$$
the last inclusion because $T\subset I'_p$. So $L(I'_p)=L(\bar J)$, and the proof of Theorem 6 continues unchanged. $\square$

So the lifted basis is proved from $T\subset I'_p$ alone. A trace that missed an element cannot pass: with (a) and (b), Proposition 10 shows that its leading monomials were complete. Such a trace only costs time, like an unlucky prime.

**Replay.** For another prime $q$ the same matrices are filled with the images of the generators and of the elements already replayed. No pair is formed and no monomial is searched. Each matrix must give the number of new elements of the trace, with the same leading columns. One combination more than that number is reduced, and it must depend on the others; a matrix with few rows has its rows reduced instead, which gives its rank. A coefficient outside the terms of the trace must vanish. A prime that fails one of these tests is dropped: the matrix has another rank modulo $q$, or a term of the trace vanished modulo $p$. If every replay shows a larger rank, the trace prime lost an element and another one is taken. The replays only supply residues for the lift. The certificate does not rely on them.

The primes are replayed on separate threads. The first batches have 2, 4, 8 and 16 primes, as in the [Buchberger replay](#72-trace-replay-lifting). Later ones have 16, or one prime per thread. A batch with fewer primes than threads shares the threads among the blocks of each prime. A replay costs about as much as the elimination of the trace, while the [replay program](#72-trace-replay-lifting) repeats only the reductions of the useful pairs. For lexicographic and elimination orders, where the trace is short and the primes are many, that program stays the cheaper one.

**Measurements.** `atlas_groebner_f4_profile` runs the trace and a number of replays alone, and returns the basis modulo the trace prime. `atlas_groebner_use_f4(0)` selects the Buchberger trace for comparison, and `atlas_groebner_modular_route` tells which trace the last basis used. [Katsura measurements](../benchmarks/groebner-f4.md)

<a id="76-generators-that-are-not-homogeneous-the-direct-trace"></a><a id="the-direct-trace"></a>

### Generators that are not homogeneous: the direct trace

**Why.** Homogenizing serves the [certificate](#73-certification), and it has a price. The ideal $I'$ of the homogenized generators is contained in the homogenization of $I$, and can be smaller: an element may only enter after a power of $y$ multiplies it. Its Gröbner basis then has more elements than that of $I$. For the Reimer system in 7 variables it has 2587 elements and 5.0 million terms modulo a prime, against 227 elements and 195 thousand terms for $I$. This happens when the homogenized generators are no complete intersection, which the bound of Lemma 9 shows: a degree misses it.

**Detection.** When the generators are not all homogeneous, the F4 trace is given up at the first degree that misses its Hilbert bound, or at once when it has no bound. Homogeneous generators, and generators whose homogenizations meet the bound in every degree, keep the F4 trace. For the others two traces are made modulo one prime: the direct one below, and the F4 trace without its bound, allowed at most twice the recorded structure of the direct one. They show what each route would lift and certify. The direct route is taken when

- the homogenized trace exceeds that allowance, or
- its reduced basis has at least one and a half times the terms of the reduced basis of $I$, and the elements of the direct trace have at most sixteen times the terms of that basis.

The second condition keeps the F4 trace where the direct certificate would cost more than it saves. It lifts every element of the trace, and their coefficients can be far larger than those of the basis. When $I$ is the unit ideal the basis is $1$, and the homogenized route proves it from a few small elements. A direct lift that is not stable after 255 primes is given up, and the F4 trace then runs to its end.

**The direct trace.** The matrices are those of the F4 trace, for the generators themselves. The pairs are taken by the degree of their least common multiple, so an element of lower degree that a matrix produces is used at once by the next one. There is no Hilbert bound: the combinations of a matrix are counted by their dependence (Lemma 8). Once the number $k$ of new elements of a matrix is known, its rows to reduce are reduced themselves, a few blocks at a time, until $k$ of them have independent images under $N$. These *selected rows* are recorded. New leading monomials of one matrix may divide one another; the reduced basis keeps the minimal ones.

**Replay and lift.** As in the F4 trace. Every element the trace inserted is replayed and lifted, not only the reduced basis: the certificate needs them.

**Certificate.** Let $p$ be the trace prime, $e_1,\dots,e_r$ the lifted elements in the order of the trace, and $G$ the lifted reduced basis. All are monic with denominators prime to $p$, and reduce modulo $p$ to the elements of the trace.

**Proposition 11.** Suppose that

1. in every matrix, each selected row reduces to zero over $\mathbf Q$ by the pivot rows of that matrix and its new elements, the rows being formed from the generators and the $e_j$;
2. each element of $G$ is its element $e_j$ minus a combination of the pivot rows of the last matrix;
3. every S-polynomial of $G$ that the criteria of section 2 keep reduces to zero by $G$;
4. every generator reduces to zero by $G$.

Then $G$ is the reduced Gröbner basis of $I$.

*Proof.* Every row of a matrix is a multiple of a generator or of an earlier element. Assume the earlier elements lie in $I$; then so do the rows. Let $N_{\mathbf Q}$ be the reduction by the pivot rows over $\mathbf Q$, $W$ the span of the $N_{\mathbf Q}(r)$ for the $k$ selected rows $r$, and $E$ the span of the $k$ new elements, which are independent because they are monic with distinct leading columns. By 1, $W\subset E$. The pivot rows are monic with coefficients in $\mathbf Z_{(p)}$, so each $N_{\mathbf Q}(r)$ has coefficients in $\mathbf Z_{(p)}$ and reduces modulo $p$ to the image of the row of the trace. Those $k$ images are independent, so a minor of size $k$ is nonzero modulo $p$ and therefore nonzero: $\dim W=k$. Hence $W=E$. Each new element is thus $N_{\mathbf Q}$ of a combination of rows, that is, a combination of rows of the matrix, and lies in $I$. By induction all $e_j$ lie in $I$, and by 2 so does $G$. By 4, $I\subset(G)$. So $(G)=I$, and by 3 and Buchberger's criterion $G$ is a Gröbner basis of it. The engine returns its reduced form, which is unique. $\square$

The proof uses no grading, and it does not need the trace to be complete. If the trace prime lost rank in a matrix, or the trace missed an element, 1 or 3 fails and another prime is taken. Condition 1 costs one exact reduction for each element of the trace. The pairs that gave nothing are not reduced again over $\mathbf Q$; they only enter through 3, for the reduced basis.

The exact reductions are made without fractions: a row is scaled by what the leading coefficient of a pivot row lacks, and its content is removed after every eighth scaling. They run on all threads.

**What keeps the F4 trace without the comparison.** A complete intersection with solutions at infinity meets the bound in every degree, although its homogenized basis is larger than the basis of $I$. For the cyclic system in 5 variables it has 38 elements against 20.

## The Hilbert-driven route

Changing to lexicographic or elimination order can produce many pairs whose S-polynomials reduce to zero. The Hilbert function tells us how many independent leading terms to expect in each degree. Once the known leading terms account for that degree, Atlas can skip its remaining pairs. This is the idea of Traverso's Hilbert-driven Buchberger algorithm. [Carlo Traverso, *Hilbert Functions and the Buchberger Algorithm* (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1006/jsco.1996.0056)

Atlas works with the homogeneous ideal $I'=(f_1^h,\dots,f_s^h)\subset S=\mathbf Q[x,y]$, comparing degrees first and breaking ties with the requested order. [Homogenization](#homogenization) · [Implementation](hilbert.cpp)

<a id="81-the-hilbert-series"></a>

### The Hilbert series

First the reduced Gröbner basis $G_0$ of $I'$ for the graded reverse lexicographic order is computed by the engine using [Buchberger’s algorithm](#gröbner-bases) or the [modular route](#the-modular-route) when coefficients swell. By Macaulay's theorem $S/I'$ and $S/L(I')$ have the same Hilbert function, and $L(I')$ is generated by the leading monomials of $G_0$. So the Hilbert series of $S/I'$ is that of a monomial ideal.

For a monomial ideal $M$ in $m$ variables write the series of $S/M$ as $N_M(t)/(1-t)^m$. `hilbert_numerator` computes $N_M$ by the recursion

$$N_M=N_{M+(x)}+t\,N_{M:x}$$

for a variable $x$ dividing at least two minimal generators. It comes from the exact sequence $0\to (S/(M:x))(-1)\xrightarrow{\ x\ }S/M\to S/(M+(x))\to0$. The recursion ends at ideals whose minimal generators are pairwise coprime; these form a regular sequence, and $N_M=\prod(1-t^{\deg})$. The tests compare the result with a direct count of standard monomials.

<a id="82-the-computation"></a>

### The computation

`Engine::compute_homogeneous` runs [Buchberger’s algorithm](#gröbner-bases) on the $f_k^h$, degree by degree. When it enters degree $d$, it computes

$$c_d=\dim (S/L)_d-\dim (S/I')_d,$$

where $L$ is generated by the leading monomials found so far. Every element inserted in degree $d$ has a leading monomial of degree $d$ outside $L$, so it lowers $c_d$ by one. When $c_d=0$ the pairs and generators of degree $d$ that remain are dropped: an element of $I'$ of degree $d$ that is its own remainder would have a leading monomial of degree $d$ in $L(I')$ but not in $L$, and there is none.

<a id="83-the-certificate"></a>

### The certificate

The result does not depend on that bookkeeping being right.

**Theorem 7.** Let $I'$ be a homogeneous ideal of $S$ and $G$ a finite set of homogeneous elements of $I'$. If the monomial ideal generated by the leading monomials of $G$ has the Hilbert series of $I'$, then $G$ is a Gröbner basis of $I'$.

*Proof.* The ideal $L_G$ generated by the leading monomials of $G$ is contained in $L(I')$. In each degree $d$ these are subspaces of $S_d$ of the same codimension, namely $\dim(S/I')_d$, by hypothesis for the first and by Macaulay's theorem for the second. So they are equal. $\square$

The elements the engine produces are combinations of the $f_k^h$, so they lie in $I'$. `hilbert_basis` computes the numerator of the monomial ideal of their leading monomials and compares it with the one obtained from $G_0$; if they differed, the route would report failure and the engine would go on to the modular route. Then Lemma 5 gives a Gröbner basis of $I$ for the given order, and the engine reduces it.

The Hilbert series used is exact, because $G_0$ is a certified basis. A Hilbert function taken from a computation modulo one prime would be faster to obtain, but it can be too large when the prime is unlucky, and pairs would then be dropped that should not be.
